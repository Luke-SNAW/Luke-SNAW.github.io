If you opened this expecting a magic checklist, an automated posting schedule, or a trick to hack the Substack algorithm, you are going to be disappointed.

The reality of Substack is simple: it is not a technical puzzle. It is just software driven by human intuition, connection, and **a lot of luck**.

## 1: The Primacy of Craft (You Have to Be a Good Writer)

### 1.1 The Wall

Here is the number one truth that growth coaches leave out of their funnels: **You have to get good at writing.** There is no way around this wall. No growth hack, perfect posting schedule, or formatting trick will save an essay that has nothing interesting to say.

When I started on Substack a year ago, I already had some writing practice. Years back, during my university days, I ran a free WordPress site where I wrote deep-dives about music. It never gained an audience and it never made a dime, but I loved doing it.

My Substack was created with a singular, selfish intent: to serve as a clean place for my thoughts to live outside of my own head. Audience growth was completely secondary; practicing the craft was the entire point.

If you take only one directive away from this entire playbook, let it be this: **No amount of algorithm gaming or daily consistency can fix bad writing.**

### 1.2 Writing Etiquette

Most people who start a newsletter are fully literate. They know how to form sentences, but they don’t understand why other people’s work sounds punchy and professional while their own text feels heavy, slow, and amateur.

The difference isn’t talent. It’s writing etiquette. Here is the exact checklist of what to do and what to ruthlessly cut out to fix your reader retention.

#### 1. Stop Talking to a Stadium

- **Mistake:** Writing to a crowd. Beginners use phrases like _“Hey guys,”_ _“Welcome back everyone,”_ or _“As many of you know.”_ This immediately dilutes the connection.
- **Rule:** Write to one single person. Use the word **“You.”** When someone opens your newsletter on their phone or laptop, they are reading it alone. Your text should feel like a private, direct letter sent from one friend straight to another. Talk to the reader as an individual, not as a metric on a dashboard.

#### 2. Kill Your Adverbs

- **Mistake:** Propping up weak, boring verbs with adverbs (words ending in “-ly”). Phrases like _“he ran quickly,”_ _“she argued passionately,”_ or _“I strictly believe.”_ \* **The Rule:** Cut the adverbs and use a heavier verb. Adverbs usually show that you don’t trust your own writing. Instead of _“ran quickly,”_ write _“sprinted.”_ Instead of _“argued passionately,”_ write _“demanded.”_ Strong, singular verbs give your text immediate authority.

#### 3. Stop Using Passive Voice

- **Mistake:** Letting your sentences be acted upon instead of taking action. For example: _“The article was noticed by Michael Burry,”_ or _“The mistake was made by the team.”_ This creates heavy, slow prose.
- **Rule:** Use active voice. Make the subject do the heavy lifting. Write: _“Michael Burry noticed the article,”_ or _“The team made a mistake.”_ Active voice creates momentum and pulls the reader down the page.

#### 4. Keep It Simple and Visual

- **The Sentences:** Long, winding sentences confuse people. Keep them brief. Give the reader’s brain a break.
- **The Language:** Don’t use fancy, high-level words just to look smart. If you use a word like _“utilize”_ when you could just say _“use,”_ you lose the reader. Write exactly how you talk in real life when you’re explaining a smart idea to a friend at a bar.
- **The Imagery:** Abstract ideas don’t stick. Don’t just talk about general “economic hardship.” Talk about _“shoveling dirt for $10 a day.”_ Give the reader concrete images they can actually picture in their mind.

#### 5. The 10% Editing Rule

The editing process is simple math: **Second Draft = First Draft – 10%**. When you sit down to edit your draft, your job isn’t to add words to look sophisticated.

Your job is to locate the “warm-up fluff” at the beginning, the repetitive sentences in the middle, and the safe, boring filler you used while your brain was getting up to speed, and ruthlessly cut it out.

If a sentence doesn’t actively move your point forward, it is actively killing your retention.

### 1.4 Spacing as Visual Pacing

Digital reading is highly visual. If a subscriber opens your email and sees a massive, unbroken wall of dense text, their brain treats it like schoolwork and they will close the tab instantly. You have to use spacing as visual punctuation.

- **Do Not Copy the LinkenIn Robots:** Avoid the artificial style of making every single sentence its own line. It looks cheap, robotic, and desperate for attention.
- **Vary Your Paragraph Length:** Create a rhythm on the page. Use a short, punchy sentence to speed up the reader’s pace. Then, follow it with a three-to-four-sentence paragraph when you need them to slow down, anchor their attention, and take in a more complex idea. The visual white space on the screen gives the reader room to breathe.

### 1.5 Learning the Craft via Osmosis (The Power of Reading)

You cannot become a high-signal writer if you only consume short-form internet garbage. You can’t write clean prose if your brain is fed on a strict diet of short tweets, corporate LinkedIn posts, and basic marketing blogs.

A massive portion of writing etiquette cannot be taught through a checklist, it has to be absorbed through **osmosis**.

When you read exceptional fiction, classic literature, or great long-form journalism, you are subconsciously downloading formatting, rhythm, structure, and tone directly into your brain.

You begin to instinctively understand how a story flows, how tension is built, and how a sentence lands with real impact.

If you read shallow content, you will write shallow content. If you read deeply researched, beautifully paced books, you will naturally start executing those exact same reflexes when you sit down in front of a blank keyboard.

#### Recommended Reading List for learning how to write:

- _**On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft**_ **by Stephen King** (The ultimate masterclass on the operational habits of writing).
- _**The Copywriter’s Handbook**_ **by Robert Bly** (The definitive guide on clear, persuasive, action-driven text).
- _**Confessions of an Advertising Man**_ **by David Ogilvy** (The blueprint for writing copy that commands human attention).
- _**[The Shapes of Stories](https://youtu.be/GOGru_4z1Vc?si=rEPKyWwNb-B_uS5O)**_ **[by Kurt Vonnegut](https://youtu.be/GOGru_4z1Vc?si=rEPKyWwNb-B_uS5O)** (A short lecture series mapping out the timeless structures of human storytelling).

## 2: Titles, Hooks, and Pacing

### 2.1 The 800 - 2,000-Word Sweet Spot

An essay needs to be long enough to fully explain a complex idea, but short enough to respect the reader’s time. Through trial and error, I found the sweet spot to be around **1800 words**, published **once a week**.

While longer posts can go viral, 800-2000 words gives you enough room to build a great story arc that hits hard and ends cleanly before the reader gets bored.

do not treat word count as a vanity metric

If a concept can be cleanly executed in 900 words, stretching it to 2,000 just to hit an arbitrary goal is actively adding the “warm-up fluff” we promised to kill.

Your metric shouldn’t be length; it should be intellectual density.

### 2.2 How to Structure Your Essay

If you want a reader to finish your essay without closing the tab, you should map it out like a mini **Hero’s Journey**:

1. **The Calm Story:** Start far away from the technical theory. Begin with a simple, relatable human story or historical example. This gives the reader a low-overhead, easy entry point into your text so they can get used to your voice.
2. **The Plunge into Despair:** Once the reader is comfortable, turn things upside down. Introduce a major problem, an unfortunate truth, or a paradox that shatters that calm story. Drop them into the pit of the issue. This creates immediate tension, making it almost impossible for them to scroll away because they want to know the solution.
3. **The Solution:** Do not leave your reader in the pit. This is where you bring in your framework, your technical advice, or the core rule (like Price’s Law) as the tool to save them. Break down the concept clearly, showing them how to fix the problem.
4. **The Higher Ground:** Finish your newsletter at a higher point than where it started. The reader should close the tab feeling like they learned something valuable and are better equipped to face the world.

### 2.3 Small Formatting Rules That Matter

- **Kill the Warm-Up Intro:** The biggest mistake beginners make is spending the first three paragraphs explaining _why_ they decided to write the post or giving unneeded background. Cut that out. Drop the reader straight into the middle of the action or a problem.
- **Fix Your Spacing:** Already talked about this, im repeating because its that important. The images shown previously in 2.4 show this very well
- **A/B Title Testing:** Substack lets you test two different headlines. Do not use this to write cheap clickbait. Use it to learn what people actually care about. Test a title that promises a _clear solution_ against a title that highlights an _interesting paradox_, and see which one your audience actually respects.

## 3: The Distribution Funnel (Notes and High-Signal Commenting)

### 3.1 The Three Layers of Feed Real Estate

On Substack, you have exactly three ways to appear on someone’s screen: your Newsletter, your Notes, and your Comments.

[The Three Layers](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!z2TL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F73255245-1bab-4ce6-bfdc-71b35b34ec73_1600x900.png)

### 3.2 Notes as Your Distributed Thought-Testers

If your newsletter is your main intelligence center, Notes are your raw proving ground. Because Notes appear on a “For You” feed, they bypass your subscriber list and put your mind directly in front of strangers.

#### The Validation Loop (Listening, Not Gaming)

Keep your Notes short—usually under two paragraphs. Do not write them to chase viral clout. Use them as a low-overhead conversation starter to test your raw insights.

If a casual, three-sentence observation generates a real, emotional response in the replies, it means you hit a genuine human chord. You immediately take that validated spark and expand it into your next 2,000-word long-form essay.

### 3.3 The Three Note Blueprints

> ⚠️ _Do not copy these text structures word-for-word like a robot. If your text reads like a growth-hack script, the modern reader will smell the corporate marketing fluff and dismiss you. Use these models strictly as training wheels to understand structural pacing. The actual raw thoughts must be 100% yours._

#### Type 1: Awareness Notes (Top of Funnel)

- **The Human Truth:** This is a broad-net, highly relatable observation about your space. It isn’t clickbait. It’s a clean statement about an everyday reality that makes a scrolling reader pause and think, _“Wow, someone finally said it.”_

- **The Structural Blueprint:** State a common, over-complicated obsession in your broad niche. Break it down with a simple, grounded human reality… Deliver a punchy closing thought.

#### Type 2: Education Notes (Middle of Funnel)

- **The Value Drop:** This shows you actually possess real-world utility. You take an established concept, a piece of history, or an economic law and break it down simply. You aren’t showing off; you are being helpful.

- **The Structural Blueprint:** Introduce a specific event or framework. Strip away the textbook jargon. Explain exactly how it applies to the reader’s life today in 1–2 simple sentences.

#### Type 3: Conviction Notes (Bottom of Funnel)

- **The Honest Filter:** This is your raw, uncompromised perspective on your niche. It is explicitly designed **not** to get mass engagement. It intentionally repels casual scrollers who want easy answers, while deeply connecting with the exact high-signal subscribers who see the world the same way you do.

- **The Structural Blueprint:** Locate a piece of soft, popular advice that you hate. Share the uncomfortable, blunt reality. Force the reader to make an honest choice between real substance and empty shortcuts.

### 3.4 The Multiplier Effect: One Essay, Three Notes

You should never sit in front of a blank screen trying to manufacture “content” for Notes. That is how people end up writing fake, engineered slop.

When you publish your weekly essay, you have already done the hard thinking. Your essay naturally holds all the raw material you need for the week. You are just sharing different windows into the exact same house.

- **The Awareness Note:** Extract the simple, everyday story or vignette you used to open your essay.
- **The Education Note:** Take the core rule or lesson from the middle of your essay and summarize it cleanly.
- **The Conviction Note:** Take the underlying belief or frustration that made you want to write the essay in the first place and share it directly.

This isn’t gaming a system. It’s making sure your ideas get full mileage instead of dying 24 hours after you hit send.

### The Middle Game: Finding Your Rhythm in the 100-to-2,000 Subscriber Dip

The most challenging stretch of this journey isn’t starting, and it isn’t managing a massive audience once you’ve arrived. It’s that quiet space in the middle.

When you have fewer than a couple hundred subscribers, your Notes won’t naturally catch fire because the network effects haven’t kicked in yet.

It’s completely normal to feel a bit invisible here. Most writers step away during this phase because they assume their strategy is broken.

It’s usually not broken at all; they just haven’t figured out how to use the quiet phase to train their creative muscles.

If you are stuck in this dip, here is exactly how to run the engine day-to-day:

- **Use the Feed as an Ideation Sandbox:** Set aside about 30 minutes a day to browse Notes and comments, but do it entirely for idea generation. See what sparks your curiosity, what makes you dynamic, or what gaps other people are leaving wide open. Use their energy to feed your own notebook.
- **Ship Fast to Kill the Doubt:** The best way to train your creative circuits is to ship a Note the exact second an idea pops into your head. Don’t overthink it, and don’t hold it hostage trying to make it perfect. If an idea turns out to be flat, it will disappear into the feed within a few hours anyway. Nobody is going to scroll through 30 pages of history to find a cringe post you made when you only had two subscribers. Let the feed swallow the misses.
- **The 3-Month Verbatim Rule:** When an idea _does_ work, you own that real estate. Do not be afraid to present that exact same insight using different words a few weeks later. In fact, if a Note does incredibly well, you can literally repost it verbatim three months later. If you’ve grown by a few hundred or a few thousand subscribers since then, you actually owe it to your new audience to share the older ideas that resonated most. They haven’t seen them yet.

### 3.5 High-Signal Commenting: The Rules of Net-Giving

Leaving comments on larger publications is the fastest way to grow a new page from zero. But if you do it with a transaction mindset, it backfires completely.

To make this work, you must apply the philosophy of being a **net giver**: your personal brand is just the sum of the value you give away for free without keeping score.

#### The Commenting Blacklist (What to Aggressively Ignore)

- **The Empty Praiser:** Writing _“Great post!”_ or _“Love this!”_ is useless. It’s conversational white noise. It adds no value to the reader or the author.
- **The Thread Hijacker:** Writing _“I actually wrote a whole breakdown on this on my page, come check it out…”_ is immediate social suicide. It looks desperate, spammy, and cheap. The author will delete it, and readers will actively avoid you.

#### The Net-Giver Mechanics

Instead, treat the reply section of a prominent publication as prime editorial real estate. Find creators completely outside your immediate category. Pick one specific point the author made, and contribute to the space by writing a miniature, standalone insight in the replies.

1. **Acknowledge the Nuance:** Show you actually read the text and understand the author’s point.
2. **Add Grounded Value:** Bring in a simple real-world example, a quick counter-intuitive historical point, or a highly visual analogy that builds on top of their original thought.
3. **Clean Exit:** End with a sharp sentence and leave. Do not pitch your link.

_This is a comment I left on an article, and it got a lot of engagement_

When you consistently provide unpretentious, high-signal value in the replies without asking for anything in return, you aren’t gaming anything.

You are earning attention. Strangers reading the thread will naturally respect your brain, click your profile picture, and navigate back to your free page on their own accord.

You lease the traffic of giant accounts by simply being the most helpful person in the comments.

### 3.5b Organic Recommendations

While Notes and comments put you in front of scrolling strangers, Substack’s most powerful growth tool happens automatically behind the scenes: The Recommendation Feature.

When another writer recommends your page, Substack suggests your newsletter to their new readers the exact second they sign up.

We already talked about why cheap, transactional shout-out swaps make you look desperate. To get respected writers to recommend you, you have to build real rapport.

If you want to completely ruin your reputation and clutter your page, follow this exact checklist:

What NOT to Do When Trying to Earn Recommendations:

- Don’t try to game the algorithm: Do not use recommendations as a hollow growth hack. Only recommend publications that you genuinely enjoy reading yourself. If you don’t actually care about the content, your readers will notice.
- Don’t slide into DMs for blind swaps: Cold-messaging people you don’t know to ask for recommendation swaps is incredibly cringe. At the very least, interact like a normal person and build some actual rapport first before you even think about making an ask.
- Don’t recommend a massive list: Do not recommend too many Substacks. I have seen writers recommending 20+ publications on their profile. At that point, you aren’t curating quality anymore, you are just soliciting random links to subscribers who originally signed up just to read your work. Keep it tight and highly selective.

**The Golden Rule:** Be a net-giver first. Go ahead and recommend other Substack pages you love, completely out of pocket and without expecting a single thing in return. When you actively champion good work without keeping score, people notice.

Build the rapport naturally by being normal and helpful, and if the alignment is obviously there, you can make a clean, direct ask.

When two writers genuinely respect each other’s craft, sharing the traffic happens as a natural byproduct.

### 3.6 Writing Headers for Scrolling Humans

Most writers title their essays like they are submitting a boring paper to a school board. They use vague, poetic phrases or dry titles that don’t mean anything to someone scrolling by.

On a busy feed, your title is 90% of the battle. If your title is soft, nobody clicks. If nobody clicks, your funnel breaks before it even starts. You have to flip your titles from _topic-focused_ to _outcome-driven_.

#### The Shift: Cutting the nonsense

- **The Poetic Mistake:** _“Sifting Through the Noise”_ or _“Reflections on the Modern Landscape.”_
  - **What they did wrong:** They valued looking artsy over being clear. Nobody knows what this actually means when they see it sitting on a fast-moving feed. It gives the reader zero incentive to stop scrolling, so they just fly right past it.
- **The Dry Textbook Mistake:** _“An Analysis of High-Growth Newsletter Models in 2026.”_
  - **What they did wrong:** They treated their newsletter like an academic chore instead of an engaging conversation. It’s boring, stiff, sounds exactly like a homework assignment, and completely kills any natural human curiosity.
- **The Turned-Up, Honest Style:** _“I Analyzed 1,000 Short Posts. Here Are the 3 Simple Layouts That Actually Get Free Sign-ups.”_
  - **Why it works:** It uses an exact, real-world metric, promises a specific shortcut to skip a major headache, and sounds like an honest text from a smart friend who just figured something out.

#### The 3 Simple Rules of a Good Title:

1. **Use Numbers or Specific Details:** Put clear numbers, timeframes, or specific metrics right in the headline. Numbers break up lines of text and force a scrolling human eye to stop.
2. **Show the Value Instantly:** Tell the reader exactly what headache they get to skip by spending five minutes reading your post.
3. **Write Like a Real Person:** Never sound like an SEO robot. Write the title exactly how you would text a smart friend when you’re sending them the answer to a problem they’ve been losing sleep over.

### 3.7 The Low-Hanging Fruit: Packaging and Your First Impression

Before someone even reads your first sentence, they look at your packaging. Substack gives you some great tools to make your page look good, and ignoring them is just leaving money on the table.

This isn’t about turning your page into a fake corporate LinkedIn profile, it’s just basic digital hygiene.

#### 1. The Profile Picture and Bio

Get a clean, high-quality profile picture. It sounds obvious, but a good photo gives you massive first-impression points. Your bio and “About” page shouldn’t be super vague or deeply poetic either. Tell people exactly who you are, what you look at, and why they should care. If a stranger lands on your page and can’t figure out your angle in five seconds, they will leave.

#### 2. Maintaining an Aesthetic (Without Wasting Time)

Substack lets you customize a lot of your publication’s website.

When you pick your header images for your articles, try to keep a consistent theme. Having a good-looking, clean page builds trust.

Go into your dashboard, play with the settings, and find a look you like. But listen to me: **for the love of all that is green on earth, do not spend 20 hours trying to pick a color palette.**

Messing with fonts and hex codes is just a fancy way to procrastinate. Set it up cleanly in an hour, and then get back to writing articles that are actually worth reading.

#### 3. The Welcome Email: Your Only Automated Asset

The moment someone enters their email address, Substack sends them an automated welcome message. This is prime real estate that most writers waste by leaving the default template active.

- **What to Avoid:** A boring message that just says: _“Thanks for subscribing to my newsletter! Look out for my next post in your inbox.”_
- **What to Do Instead:** Write a short, warm, and direct letter. Tell them what your publication stands for, what days you send your main articles and drop a link to 2 or 3 of your absolute best past articles. Give them instant value right when they walk through the front door.

### 3.8 The Macro Algorithm Law: Platforms Reward Presence, Not Just Brilliance

Amateurs love to complain about “the algorithm.” They treat it like a hidden enemy trying to suppress their art.

They think, _“I just spent three weeks writing a 5,000-word masterpiece that could literally bring about world peace. Why did the software let it die with twelve views?”_

Here is the cold reality of how all media networks work: **They don’t care about the philosophical merit of your ideas. They care about keeping people on the platform.**

#### The Content-Dip Fallacy

If you write one massive, earth-shattering article and then vanish for a month, you are actively working against the platform’s model.

Substack wants active, consistent people on its network.

Active accounts keep users inside the app, start real conversations, and drive growth. When you “publish and dip,” you break the loop.

The system realizes you aren’t a reliable source of reader attention, and it stops showing your work to strangers on the feed.

#### The Ecosystem Exchange Rate

To scale your publication, you have to accept the real exchange rate of the internet. You can’t just pull attention out of a community without putting active energy back into it.

- **The Transactional Mistake:** _“I put my writing out there. Now the system owes me an audience.”_
- **The Real Operator Mindset:** _“I am using Notes and Comments every week to actively help this place stay interesting and valuable. In exchange, the platform will route new readers to my main page.”_

You don’t need micro-hacks, loop tricks, or weird engagement groups to win. You win by understanding that consistent, high-value presence is the only way to show a network you are worth recommending.

#### The Collaboration Multiplier

Because networks favor activity and movement, collaborating with other writers is your highest-leverage growth tool.

When you do a cross-post, recommend another writer, or share a thread with someone in your broad niche, you aren’t just swapping readers.

You are signaling to the system that your page is an active hub where real people are talking. The system notices that multi-account traffic and naturally pushes your work wider.

You don’t beat the software by out-smarting it; you beat it by being the most consistently helpful writer inside it.

## 4: Ecosystem Strategy (The Broad Niche Framework)

### 4.1 The Total Addressable Market (TAM) Illusion

Every generic internet growth guru will tell you the exact same thing: _“You have to pick a hyper-specific, microscopic niche if you want to make money.”_

They are dead wrong. If you pick a niche that is too small, you cap your audience before you even write your first sentence.

Even worse, you sign a creative death warrant. You trap yourself in a tiny room where you will run out of things to say within three months.

### 4.2 Case Study: How Zaha Trapped Her Own Mind

To understand how this trap works in the real world, let’s look at a writer named Zaha.

Zaha teaches yoga at a local studio in Budapest. She decided to launch a Substack newsletter with a very specific business goal: she wanted to get more local clients into her physical studio classes. Naturally, she followed the standard internet advice and picked a hyper-localized, hyper-specific niche. She titled her publication _Budapest Yoga_.

For the first two months, she wrote exact, practical articles about yoga postures, breathing exercises, and class schedules in Budapest.

#### The Wall

Quickly, Zaha hit a wall. She got incredibly bored.

Her real intellectual obsession wasn’t just physical posturing—it was the deeper, esoteric side of the practice.

She wanted to write about chakras, mental energy, the psychology of human love, and raw personal stories about how meditation saved her during her darkest personal moments.

But she felt stuck. She couldn’t write an intense, deep essay about spiritual energy loops under a newsletter titled _Budapest Yoga_ without confusing the local clients who just signed up to learn how to stretch their hamstrings.

By choosing a tiny niche, she locked herself out of her own publication.

[pic](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!EfhA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F59f30a95-516f-4fd5-bcf7-7b38af41182c_1408x768.png)

#### The Fix: The Broad Niche Framework

Zaha didn’t need to scrap her business goals. She just needed to zoom out.

Instead of naming her publication after a physical geographic location and a single physical activity, she should have positioned her work under a Broad Niche: _Meditative Yoga & Philosophy_.

(And to be clear, “**Budapest Yoga**” is still a perfectly fine name for a local studio, but we are using it here to make a point about scale, i actually prefer it to Meditative yoga and philosophy since its shorter and more concise).

This simple shift changes everything:

1. **She can still get clients:** She can easily drop a quick note at the bottom of her free essays inviting local Budapest readers to come to her studio.
2. **She has endless creative freedom:** Because the umbrella is broad, she can seamlessly write about chakras, spiritual energy, and philosophy one week, and practical stretching routines the next. It is close enough to the core topic that readers don’t feel alienated, but wide enough that Zaha never gets bored.

When you are first starting out, changing your publication name or tweaking your niche is easy because you don’t have a massive audience that signed up for one specific thing.

Use that to your advantage. Experiment ruthlessly at the beginning, because your room to play shrinks the larger your audience grows.

### 4.3 Is Your Niche a Prison?

Before you write your next post, sit down and answer these 2 questions honestly. Don’t think like a marketer; think like a human being who has to write this newsletter every single week for the next three years.

1. **The 6-Month Boredom Test:** If you had to write 25 separate, 2,000-word articles _only_ about the exact title of your newsletter right now, would you want to scream by week 12?
2. **The Dinner Party Test:** When you are hanging out with friends and get deeply excited talking about your field, are you talking about the dry technical mechanics (like Zaha’s stretching routines), or are you talking about the deeper, weirder principles behind it (like the philosophy and energy)? Your newsletter needs to match the things that actually keep you awake at night.

**The Rule:** Choose a massive pasture, but keep a very distinct, unique perspective within it. Give your mind room to evolve, because a bored writer will always create a boring newsletter.

### 4.4 The Personal PR Boundary

Most people have a completely warped idea of what a “personal brand” actually is. Let’s strip away the corporate buzzwords. A personal brand is simply **Personal Public Relations (PR)**.

It is the exact same tool a massive corporation uses to manage its public reputation, just scaled down to an individual mind.

When people hear the word “authenticity,” they usually panic and make one of two critical mistakes:

1. **The Over-Sharer Trap (Cringe TMI):** They think being authentic means showing everything. They write long, uncomfortable posts detailing their morning routine, their daily anxieties, or a random fight they had with a neighbor. It feels messy, desperate, and forces the reader into an awkward parasocial relationship.
2. **The Corporate Robot Trap (Cringe Polish):** They swing too far the other way. They try to look hyper-professional by using sanitized, safe language and posting clean, soulless updates that sound like a corporate human resources memo. It has zero human warmth.

#### The House Guest Rule

To find the perfect balance between being a real human and keeping your professional edge, use the **House Guest Rule**.

Think of your personal brand like inviting a guest over to your actual home for dinner:

> If you invite someone over, you clean up the living room. You wipe down the counters, throw away the trash, and put on some good music. You don’t leave your dirty laundry on the couch just to prove that you are “authentic” and real. That’s just sloppy.
>
> At the same time, you don’t rent a sterile, million-dollar Airbnb for the night, pretend it’s your house, and act like a robot butler. That’s fake, and your guest will smell the lie instantly.

Your Substack page is your digital home. When you publish a **Conviction Note** or a personal story, you are letting people into your living room.

You should absolutely share real personal experiences, your genuine struggles, and how your brain actually functions. But you present it with dignity, focus, and a clean layout.

Show them your real home, but clean up the trash before they walk through the front door. Don’t bore people with the microscopic, messy details of your daily life, and don’t lie to them with a corporate mask.

Be a real human who exercises high standards.

### 5: The Funnel Truth (The Real Monetization Model)

#### 5.1 The Paywall Paradox

Let’s look directly at the math: if you choose to lock **all** your content behind a paywall when you only have 100 or even 1,000 subscribers, you are stopping your own growth before it starts.

To get people to hand over their hard-earned money month after month just to read your text, your writing has to be lightyears better and more predictive than everything else available on the internet for free. If it isn’t, a paywall just acts as a giant “Keep Out” sign for new readers.

When you paywall **all of** your newsletter too early, you are treating the words on the page as the final product.

This forces you into a low-ticket volume game where you are constantly hustling to defend a $5 or $10 subscription to a casual crowd. You become a content landlord chasing pocket change.

#### 5.2 The Compounding Value of “Free”

The creators who actually pull off a successful paid tier don’t just block off their essays and call it a day; they build a massive foundation of **free value firs**t. When they do launch a paid tier, they treat it like an exclusive club where the writing is just the baseline, attaching actual, tangible value to the subscription:

- **Digital Resources:** Complete video courses, Notion templates, or their personal workflow systems.
- **Exclusive Perks:** Early-bird access to book releases, private Q&A calls, or a community forum.

They are selling an ecosystem of resources, not just words. But even with those perks, blocking off your best ideas is a massive strategic mistake when you’re scaling. Why? Because **paywalls kill virality.**

Think about it like this: if I had put my most viral long-form essay behind a paid paywall before it took off, it never would have spread. It would have died in darkness.

Because that article was 100% free and open to the web, anyone could share it, link to it, and restack it without friction.

That single open essay made me over $2,000 in a single month just from gifts and paid consultations.

That might not sound like a million dollars to a corporate guru, but it is significantly more than a handful of $5 paid subscriptions would have ever generated.

And that isn’t even the best part. Because that masterpiece was free for the whole world to see, it acted as a massive billboard for my brain. It triggered my best month ever for warm traffic landing straight in my DMs—founders, operators, and executives asking for my high-ticket ghostwriting and consultancy services.

The compounding value of that single piece of work was only unleashed because I removed the lock from the door.

#### 5.3 The High-Ticket Backend Menu

Instead of begging a handful of readers for the price of a cup of coffee every month, you leave the front door wide open so you can land back-door deals that actually move the needle. Here is what your free newsletter should actually be selling:

Event organizers will fly you out and pay you premium flat fees to deliver keynote talks because your free essays proved you can hold an audience. Traditional publishers and literary agents will slide into your DMs with major book deals because they can already see your ideas have market validation and a built-in audience ready to buy on day one.

These are just a few examples. The possibilities here are near endless.

Stop trying to collect pocket change at the gate when you can use your craft to command serious capital at the back door. Your newsletter isn’t the product—it’s the pipeline.

## 7: The Receipt Room (What Wasted My Time) And TLDR

### 7.1 The Wasted Weeks Checklist

The value of a premium guide isn’t just telling you what to do—it’s saving you months of wasted time by telling you what to ignore. When people get desperate for growth, they start doing weird things to try and trick the system. It backfires every time. Here is what you should stay far away from:

- **Restack Swapping:**
  - **The Problem:** This is when you message a buddy saying, _“Hey, I’ll share your short post if you share mine.”_ It is super cringe and everyone can see right through it.
  - **Why it fails:** You are just faking numbers. Smart readers notice when the same four people constantly repost each other’s work. It makes you look like a cheap internet salesman instead of a real writer.
- **Recommendation Swapping:**
  - **The Problem:** Putting another writer’s page on your profile _only_ because they promised to do the same for you.
  - **Why it fails:** Your recommendations are a promise of quality to your readers. The moment you trade them like favors, you break that trust. If you recommend a bad page just to get a favor back, people will realize your standards are low.
- **Link-Dropping in the Comments:**
  - **The Problem:** Going into the comment section of a big writer and posting a link to your own page saying, _“I wrote about this too, check it out here!”_
  - **Why it fails:** It’s like walking into someone else’s shop and yelling at their customers to come buy from you instead. It is rude, desperate, and tacky. The author will delete it, and readers will actively avoid you.
- **Over-Engineered Threads:**
  - **The Problem:** Spending hours formatting complicated, multi-part posts on Notes that read like corporate marketing scripts.
  - **Why it fails:** People don’t open the app to read robotic, over-polished sales scripts. These posts might get a few empty likes from other growth-chasers, but they bring in zero real subscribers who actually want to read your long articles.
- **Algorithm Chasing:**
  - **The Problem:** Writing about trending news, internet drama, or hype topics you don’t care about just because the platform is pushing them that day.
  - **Why it fails:** It ruins your unique voice. You end up attracting low-quality readers who only care about the trend. The moment the hype dies, they leave, leaving you with a dead list and a confused brand.
- **The “Hi Substack” Begging Post:**
  - **The Problem:** Writing short posts like, _“Hi feed, connect me with other writers who talk about business!”_
  - **Why it fails:** It treats a crowd of real humans like a machine you are trying to activate. Serious people scroll right past this because it proves you have absolutely nothing original to say on your own.

### 7.2 The Perfectionism Trap

The biggest time thief in my early days was holding my drafts hostage because I wanted them to sound perfect.

Over-editing ruins your unique voice. The modern internet reader is starved for raw, human reality.

Write it, clean up the fluff using the 10% rule, and put it out. Skill builds up over time, but only if you publish the work.

- **The Distribution Loop:** Your long essays are your main product. They must stay 100% free so they can spread across the internet without friction. To write sharp articles, you have to read deeply—old books, history, biographies, and the best writers in your space. Reading is the actual fuel for your ideas. ### 7.4 The Ultimate Do’s and Don’ts Checklist

#### The Do’s (Do This Consistently)

- **Write clear, outcome-driven headlines:** Use clear numbers and specific details so a scrolling reader instantly knows why your article is worth their time.
- **Slice your essays into short posts:** Take your weekly long article and chop it into three short notes (one for awareness, one for education, and one for your core beliefs) to save your energy.

#### The Don’ts (Avoid This Like the Plague)

- **Don’t hide your writing behind an early paywall:** Locking up your best ideas before you grow is commercial suicide. It stops your articles from going viral.
- **Don’t participate in cheap growth loops:** Stay far away from swapping reposts, swapping recommendations just for favors, and dropping links to your page in other people’s comments.
- **Don’t over-edit your unique voice:** Stop holding your drafts hostage trying to make them perfect. Clean up the fluff and hit publish.
- **Don’t spend 20 hours picking a color palette:** Fiddling with codes and fonts is just a fancy way to procrastinate. Focus on making the articles worth reading.
- **Don’t over-share your messy personal life:** Remember the House Guest Rule. Be a real human, but keep your professional edge. Clean up the living room before you invite people in.

- 90일 동안 20,585명, 월 5,000명 이상의 구독자 증가를 만든 방법은 알고리즘 공략이 아니라 글쓰기 실력, 전면 무료 배포, 운을 받을 준비의 조합이라고 정리함.
- 문장 예절(writing etiquette)이 독자 유지율을 결정하므로 한 사람에게 말하기, 부사 제거, 능동태 사용, 구체적 이미지, 2차 원고에서 10% 삭감을 실행 규칙으로 제시함.
- 뉴스레터와 Notes와 댓글이라는 세 개의 피드 지면을 하나의 유입 경로로 묶고, 주 1회 800~2,000단어 에세이를 축으로 Notes 3~5개와 고신호 댓글을 붙이는 구조를 권장함.
- 좁은 니치는 독자 수를 제한하는 동시에 창작 소재를 3개월 만에 고갈시키므로, 넓은 니치(Broad Niche) 안에서 뚜렷한 관점을 유지하라고 제안함.
- 조기 유료화는 바이럴을 죽이므로 에세이를 100% 무료로 열어두고, 고스트라이팅과 컨설팅과 강연과 출간 계약 같은 고티켓 백엔드로 수익을 만드는 모델을 제시함.
- 4개월간 50 likes에 머물던 Price's Law 에세이가 11,000 likes와 250k impressions로 확산되고 Michael Burry의 리스택을 받으면서 구독자가 2,000명에서 13,000명으로 늘어난 과정을 사례로 제시함.

## 성장 해킹은 나쁜 글을 구하지 못함

- 글쓰기 실력이라는 벽에는 우회로가 없고, 할 말이 없는 에세이는 게시 일정이나 서식 기법으로 살아나지 않음.
- 필자는 1년 전 Substack을 시작했고, 그 전에는 대학 시절 무료 WordPress 사이트에서 음악 심층 분석을 쓰며 독자도 수익도 얻지 못했음.
  - Substack은 생각을 머리 밖에 두는 깨끗한 장소로 만든 것이고, 독자 성장은 부차적 목표였음.
- 43명의 구독자가 각각 같은 질문을 DM으로 보내와 이 글을 썼다고 밝힘.

## 문장 예절이 독자 이탈을 결정함

- 군중을 향해 쓰지 않고 한 사람에게 쓰기를 요구함.
  - "Hey guys"나 "Welcome back everyone" 같은 표현은 연결을 희석시키므로 "You"를 쓰고 친구에게 보내는 사적인 편지처럼 쓸 것을 제시함.
- 부사를 없애고 무거운 동사로 대체함.
  - "ran quickly"는 "sprinted"로, "argued passionately"는 "demanded"로 바꿀 것을 예시로 제시함.
- 수동태를 능동태로 바꿔 문장에 속도를 만듦.
  - "The article was noticed by Michael Burry"를 "Michael Burry noticed the article"로 고치는 예를 제시함.
- 문장은 짧게, 단어는 일상어로, 이미지는 구체적으로 씀.
  - "utilize" 대신 "use"를 쓰고, 추상적인 "economic hardship"이 아니라 "shoveling dirt for $10 a day"처럼 그려지는 장면을 제시함.
- 10% 편집 규칙을 제시함.
  - 2차 원고는 1차 원고에서 10%를 덜어낸 결과이며, 도입부의 준비운동성 군더더기와 중반의 반복 문장을 잘라냄.

## 여백이 시각적 구두점 역할을 함

- 빽빽한 텍스트 덩어리를 본 구독자는 학교 과제처럼 받아들이고 즉시 탭을 닫음.
- 모든 문장을 한 줄씩 배치하는 LinkedIn식 서식은 값싸고 절박하게 보이므로 피할 것을 제시함.
- 단락 길이를 변주해 짧은 문장으로 속도를 올리고 3~4문장 단락으로 속도를 낮추는 리듬을 만듦.

## 깊은 독서가 글의 리듬을 몸에 새김

- 짧은 트윗과 기업용 LinkedIn 게시물과 기초적인 마케팅 블로그만 소비하면 깔끔한 산문을 쓸 수 없음.
- 뛰어난 소설과 고전 문학과 장문 저널리즘을 읽을 때 서식과 리듬과 구조와 톤이 무의식적으로 흡수됨.
- 추천 목록으로 Stephen King의 On Writing, Robert Bly의 The Copywriter's Handbook, David Ogilvy의 Confessions of an Advertising Man, Kurt Vonnegut의 The Shapes of Stories를 제시함.

## 분량이 아니라 지적 밀도가 기준임

- 시행착오를 거쳐 찾은 최적 지점은 주 1회, 약 1800단어이며 범위는 800~2,000단어임.
- 900단어로 깔끔하게 끝낼 개념을 목표치를 맞추려고 2,000단어로 늘리는 것은 군더더기를 다시 넣는 행위임.

## 에세이는 작은 영웅의 여정으로 설계함

- 평온한 이야기로 시작해 이론에서 멀리 떨어진 인간적 일화나 역사적 사례로 진입 장벽을 낮춤.
- 독자가 편안해진 뒤 문제나 역설을 던져 앞의 평온을 깨고 긴장을 만듦.
- Price's Law 같은 프레임워크를 해법으로 제시해 독자를 구덩이에서 꺼냄.
- 시작보다 높은 지점에서 끝내 독자가 배웠다는 감각을 갖고 탭을 닫게 함.
- 도입부에서 집필 동기나 불필요한 배경을 설명하는 준비운동식 서론은 삭제함.
- Substack의 두 개 헤드라인 A/B 테스트는 클릭베이트가 아니라 독자의 관심사를 배우는 용도로 쓸 것을 제시함.

## 피드에 노출되는 지면은 뉴스레터와 Notes와 댓글 세 개뿐임

- Notes는 For You 피드에 노출되므로 구독자 목록을 우회해 낯선 사람에게 도달함.
- Notes는 두 단락 이내로 짧게 유지하고 바이럴을 노리지 않는 대화 시작점으로 씀.
  - 세 문장짜리 관찰이 답글에서 감정적 반응을 만들면 그 지점을 다음 장문 에세이로 확장함.
- Notes를 세 유형으로 구분함.
  - Awareness Notes는 넓은 그물을 던지는 공감형 관찰로 스크롤을 멈추게 함.
  - Education Notes는 개념이나 역사나 경제 법칙을 1~2문장으로 실생활에 연결함.
  - Conviction Notes는 대중적 조언을 반박하는 날것의 관점으로, 대량 반응을 의도적으로 포기하고 고신호 구독자만 끌어당김.
- 구조 견본을 문구까지 그대로 복사하면 마케팅 스크립트로 읽히므로 페이싱 학습용으로만 쓸 것을 경고함.

## 에세이 한 편이 그 주의 Notes 세 개를 공급함

- 빈 화면 앞에서 콘텐츠를 제조하려 하면 가짜로 설계된 글이 나옴.
- 에세이 도입부의 일상적 일화는 Awareness Note가 됨.
- 중반의 핵심 규칙은 Education Note가 됨.
- 글을 쓰게 만든 신념이나 불만은 Conviction Note가 됨.

## 100명에서 2,000명 사이의 침묵은 전략 실패가 아님

- 구독자가 수백 명 미만이면 네트워크 효과가 작동하지 않아 Notes가 확산되지 않음.
- 하루 30분 정도 Notes와 댓글을 아이디어 채굴 목적으로만 훑을 것을 제시함.
- 아이디어가 떠오른 즉시 Notes를 발행하고, 실패한 글은 몇 시간 안에 피드에 묻히므로 붙잡지 않음.
- 3개월 재게시 규칙(3-Month Verbatim Rule)을 제시함.
  - 반응이 좋았던 Notes는 3개월 뒤 문구 그대로 다시 올릴 수 있고, 그동안 늘어난 신규 독자는 해당 글을 본 적이 없음.

## 댓글은 대가를 바라지 않는 증여로만 작동함

- 큰 퍼블리케이션의 댓글은 새 계정을 0에서 키우는 가장 빠른 경로지만 거래 심리로 접근하면 역효과가 남.
- "Great post!" 같은 빈 칭찬은 대화의 백색소음이며 아무 가치도 더하지 않음.
- 자기 페이지 링크를 남기는 행위는 즉각적인 사회적 자살이며 작성자가 삭제하고 독자가 회피함.
- 자기 영역 밖의 창작자를 찾아 특정 논점 하나를 골라 독립적으로 완결된 통찰을 답글로 남김.
  - 저자 논지의 뉘앙스를 인정하고, 실제 사례나 반직관적 역사적 사실이나 시각적 비유를 더하고, 링크 없이 깔끔하게 빠짐.

## 추천 기능은 거래가 아니라 관계로 얻음

- 다른 작가가 추천하면 그 작가의 신규 독자가 가입하는 순간 Substack이 해당 뉴스레터를 제안함.
- 직접 즐겨 읽는 퍼블리케이션만 추천하고, 성장 해킹 수단으로 쓰지 않음.
- 모르는 사람에게 콜드 DM으로 상호 추천을 요청하는 행위를 피할 것을 제시함.
- 20개 이상을 추천하는 작가는 큐레이션이 아니라 링크 살포에 가까우므로 목록을 좁게 유지함.
- 아무 대가를 기대하지 않고 먼저 추천하는 순수 증여자(net giver)가 되라고 제안함.

## 제목이 승부의 90%임

- 대부분의 작가가 학교 과제처럼 모호하거나 시적인 제목을 붙여 스크롤하는 사람에게 아무 의미도 전달하지 못함.
- "Sifting Through the Noise" 같은 시적 제목은 예술적으로 보이려다 명료함을 잃음.
- "An Analysis of High-Growth Newsletter Models in 2026" 같은 건조한 제목은 숙제처럼 읽혀 호기심을 죽임.
- "I Analyzed 1,000 Short Posts. Here Are the 3 Simple Layouts That Actually Get Free Sign-ups."는 실제 수치와 구체적 지름길과 친구의 말투를 동시에 담음.
- 숫자나 기간이나 지표를 제목에 넣어 시선을 멈추게 하고, 독자가 건너뛸 수 있는 골칫거리를 즉시 알리고, SEO 로봇이 아닌 사람의 말투로 씀.

## 페이지 포장은 취미가 아니라 기본 위생임

- 선명한 프로필 사진과 직설적인 소개문을 두고, 낯선 사람이 5초 안에 관점을 파악하지 못하면 떠난다고 지적함.
- 헤더 이미지의 테마를 일관되게 유지해 신뢰를 만듦.
- 색상 팔레트를 고르며 20시간을 쓰는 것은 세련된 형태의 미루기이므로 한 시간 안에 정리할 것을 제시함.
- 환영 메일은 유일한 자동화 자산이며 기본 템플릿을 방치하는 것이 낭비임.
  - 퍼블리케이션의 지향과 발행 요일을 알리고 최고의 과거 글 2~3편 링크를 넣어 즉각적 가치를 제공함.

## 플랫폼은 재능보다 지속적 존재를 보상함

- 네트워크는 아이디어의 철학적 가치가 아니라 사용자를 플랫폼에 머물게 하는지를 기준으로 작동함.
- 거대한 글 한 편을 낸 뒤 한 달 사라지면 시스템은 신뢰할 수 있는 관심 공급원으로 보지 않고 낯선 독자에게 노출을 멈춤.
- Notes와 댓글로 매주 커뮤니티에 에너지를 넣는 대가로 플랫폼이 신규 독자를 보내준다는 교환 관계로 이해할 것을 제시함.
- 크로스포스트와 추천과 스레드 공유는 페이지가 활동 중인 허브라는 신호를 보내 노출을 넓힘.

## 좁은 니치는 시장이 아니라 창작을 가둠

- 초소형 니치를 고르면 첫 문장을 쓰기 전에 독자 규모가 제한되고 3개월 안에 할 말이 사라짐.
- Budapest에서 요가를 가르치는 Zaha의 사례를 제시함.
  - 지역 수강생 유입을 목표로 Budapest Yoga라는 이름의 뉴스레터를 만들고 두 달간 자세와 호흡법과 수업 일정을 씀.
  - 실제 관심은 차크라와 정신적 에너지와 사랑의 심리학과 명상이 자신을 구한 경험이었으나 제목이 그것을 막았음.
  - 해법으로 Meditative Yoga & Philosophy 같은 넓은 니치 아래 배치해 지역 수강 안내를 글 하단에 붙이는 방식을 제시함.
- 독자가 적은 초기에는 이름과 니치 변경이 쉬우므로 그 시기에 실험할 것을 제안함.
- 6개월 권태 테스트(6-Month Boredom Test)를 제시함.
  - 현재 뉴스레터 제목 그대로 2,000단어 글 25편을 써야 한다면 12주째에 비명을 지르게 될지 자문함.
- 저녁 식사 테스트(Dinner Party Test)를 제시함.
  - 친구들과 흥분해서 말하는 주제가 건조한 기술적 절차인지 그 이면의 원리인지 확인함.

## 개인 브랜드는 손님맞이 규칙으로 관리함

- 개인 브랜드는 개인 PR(Personal PR)이며 기업이 쓰는 평판 관리 도구를 개인 규모로 축소한 것임.
- 과잉 공유는 아침 루틴과 일상적 불안과 이웃과의 다툼까지 노출해 어색한 준사회적 관계를 강요함.
- 기업 로봇형은 위생 처리된 언어와 인사부 메모 같은 업데이트로 인간적 온기를 없앰.
- 손님맞이 규칙(House Guest Rule)을 제시함.
  - 손님을 부르면 거실을 치우지만, 진정성을 증명하려고 빨래를 소파에 두지는 않음.
  - 동시에 무균 상태의 고급 숙소를 빌려 자기 집인 척하지도 않음.
  - 실제 경험과 고민은 공유하되 품위와 초점과 깔끔한 레이아웃으로 제시함.

## 조기 유료화는 확산을 죽임

- 구독자 100명이나 1,000명 단계에서 전체를 유료화하면 성장이 시작되기 전에 멈춤.
- 월 $5나 $10 구독을 방어하는 저단가 물량 게임에 갇혀 콘텐츠 임대업자가 됨.
- 유료 티어를 성공시킨 창작자는 무료 가치의 토대를 먼저 쌓고 실체 있는 혜택을 붙임.
  - 영상 강의와 Notion 템플릿과 개인 워크플로 시스템 같은 디지털 자원을 제시함.
  - 책 선공개와 비공개 Q&A 통화와 커뮤니티 포럼 같은 특전을 제시함.
- 가장 확산된 에세이를 유료로 잠갔다면 어둠 속에서 죽었을 것이라고 지적함.
  - 100% 무료였기 때문에 마찰 없이 공유와 링크와 리스택이 가능했음.
  - 그 글 하나로 후원과 유료 컨설팅만으로 한 달에 $2,000 이상을 벌었고, 소수의 $5 구독보다 큰 금액이라고 밝힘.

## 무료 에세이는 고티켓 백엔드로 이어지는 파이프라인임

- 고단가 고스트라이팅은 온라인 존재감을 원하지만 시간과 기술이 없는 브랜드와 임원에게 월 수천 달러 단위로 판매됨.
- 전략 자문과 컨설팅은 창업자가 특정 병목에 대한 직접적 해법을 원하기 때문에 성립하며, 무료 에세이가 매주 패턴 인식 능력을 증명함.
- 유료 강연과 출간 계약은 무료로 멀리 퍼진 아이디어가 업계 게이트키퍼와 출판사에 닿을 때 발생함.
- 뉴스레터 자체가 제품이 아니라 파이프라인이라고 정리함.

## 바이럴은 사고가 아니라 준비된 자산과 배치의 결과임

- Price's Law를 다룬 에세이는 4개월간 페이지에 놓인 채 50 likes에 머물렀음.
- 자기 니치 밖의 큰 퍼블리케이션 댓글에 독립적인 생각을 남기기 시작한 것이 계기가 됨.
- 해당 에세이는 몇 주 만에 11,000 likes와 250k impressions로 확산됨.
- The Big Short으로 알려진 투자자 Michael Burry가 "Good read"라는 두 단어와 함께 리스택함.
  - 그 한 번의 지지로 2,000 likes가 붙고 수천 명의 신규 구독자가 유입되어 한 달 안에 2,000명에서 13,000명이 됨.
- 운은 존재하지만, 수년간의 글쓰기 연습과 한 자리 수 반응에도 발행을 이어간 기간은 운이 아니었다고 정리함.
  - 영향력 있는 사람도 자기가 실제로 좋아하는 것만 공유하므로, 운이 왔을 때 글이 게으르고 레이아웃이 엉망이면 운은 사라짐.

## Price's Law 글의 구조를 요소별로 해부함

- 제목 The Mathematical Reason Most People Never "Make It"은 구체적 개념과 누구나 느껴본 감정을 결합함.
  - 부제는 노력과 결과가 일치하지 않는다는 불편한 진실을 약속해 호기심을 만듦.
- 도입부는 데이터가 아니라 Spotify Wrapped라는 공통 경험으로 시작해 진입 부담을 없앰.
  - 모두의 연말 목록에 같은 이름들이 반복 등장한다는 관찰에서 출발함.
- 핵심 프레임워크는 어떤 영역의 인원 수의 제곱근에 해당하는 사람이 전체 성과의 50%를 만든다는 Price's Law임.
  - 25명 팀에서 5명이 중심 업무를 처리하는 예시로 학술적 설명을 대체함.
- 후반부는 개념을 세 가지 실행 영역으로 분해함.
  - 콘텐츠 영역에서 수백 편 중 소수가 성장을 만든다는 실제 Substack 수치를 공개함.
  - 기술 영역에서 모든 약점을 고치기보다 상위 두어 개 강점을 결합하는 편이 낫다고 설명함.
  - 시간 영역에서 한 주의 일부만 실질적 성과를 만들고 나머지는 유지 업무라고 설명함.
- 결론은 초기에는 탐색으로 데이터를 모으고 후기에는 검증된 것을 집중 활용하라는 순서를 제시함.

## 스토리텔링이 서식 결함을 덮음

- Freya India의 Nobody Has A Personality Anymore: We are products with labels를 스토리텔링 사례로 제시함.
- 도입부에서 치료 언어(therapy-speak)를 곧바로 적으로 지목해 숨은 불만에 언어를 부여함.
- 중반은 따뜻한 인간적 묘사와 차가운 임상적 라벨을 나란히 붙여 긴장을 만듦.
  - 사랑스럽게 잘 잊는 사람이라는 표현과 ADHD라는 진단을 대조함.
- 60년간 결혼 생활을 유지한 조부모에게 왜 서로를 택했는지 물었을 때 나온 서툰 대답을 근거로 삼아 논지를 인간화함.
  - 개인사를 하소연으로 쓰지 않고 논지를 받치는 깔끔한 장면으로만 사용해 손님맞이 규칙을 지킴.
- 결말은 자기 분석을 멈추는 행위를 용기와 저항으로 규정하며 독자를 더 높은 지점에 놓음.
- 원문은 매우 크고 밀도 높은 단락을 쓰는 서식 결함을 가졌으나, 글의 질이 그것을 상쇄해 수만 건의 반응을 만들었다고 지적함.
  - 참여 유도 문구 없이 반박하고 공유하게 만드는 관점의 깊이만으로 확산됨.

## 성장에 급할 때 하는 행동들이 시간을 낭비시킴

- 리스택 교환은 같은 몇 명이 서로의 글을 반복 공유하는 것을 독자가 알아채므로 실패함.
- 추천 교환은 품질 약속을 거래로 바꿔 기준이 낮다는 사실을 드러냄.
- 댓글 링크 투척은 남의 가게에서 손님을 부르는 행위이며 삭제와 회피를 초래함.
- 기업 마케팅 스크립트처럼 과잉 설계된 다단 Notes는 성장 추종자들의 빈 반응만 얻고 실제 구독자를 데려오지 않음.
- 관심 없는 트렌드와 인터넷 논쟁을 쫓으면 목소리를 잃고, 화제가 끝나면 죽은 목록만 남음.
- 다른 작가를 연결해 달라는 요청형 게시물은 독창적으로 할 말이 없다는 사실을 증명함.
- 초고를 완벽하게 만들려고 붙잡아 두는 과잉 편집이 고유한 목소리를 지운다고 지적함.

## 핵심 엔진은 배포 루프와 백엔드 수익과 입력 엔진 세 가지로 압축됨

- 장문 에세이는 마찰 없이 퍼져야 하므로 100% 무료로 두고, Notes로 아이디어를 시험하고, 큰 계정의 댓글로 이름을 노출함.
- 무료 글이 두뇌의 광고판 역할을 해 고단가 고객과 컨설팅 문의와 강연과 출간 계약을 뒷문으로 끌어옴.
- 꾸준한 산출은 입력 없이 불가능하므로 오래된 책과 역사와 전기와 최고의 작가를 깊이 읽는 것이 아이디어의 연료라고 정리함.
- 숫자를 확인하기 전에 연속 10편을 발행하는 10편 규칙(10-Post Rule)을 제시함.

## 15분 세팅과 6개월 루틴을 실행 과제로 제시함

- 전체를 무료로 유지하더라도 첫날부터 유료 구독 옵션을 켜서 자발적 후원 경로를 열어 둠.
- Substack 생태계 내 검색과 발견이 가능하도록 설정을 확인함.
- 기본 문구를 지우고 발행 빈도를 명시한 세 문장짜리 환영 메일을 쓰고 최고 신호의 과거 글을 연결함.
- 랜딩 페이지는 배경색 하나와 읽기 쉬운 서체와 제목과 이메일 입력란만 남기고 위젯을 제거함.
- 이번 주 안에 길이와 무관하게 에세이 한 편을 발행할 것을 요구함.
- 이후 6개월간 주 1편의 장문 에세이와 주 3~5개의 Notes를 유지할 것을 제안함.
  - Notes는 큰 개념을 한 문장으로 압축하는 능력을 훈련하고, 에세이는 그 조각들을 하나의 논증으로 연결하는 능력을 훈련한다고 설명함.
